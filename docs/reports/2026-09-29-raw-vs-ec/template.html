Why the detector does better on raw bands than on EC filter responses

Trained on the 133 raw hyperspectral bands, the YOLO26s detector finds 76 % of the camouflaged objects in the test set. Fed the 344 responses of the electrochromic (EC) tunable filter instead, it finds 56 %. The data explain most of that gap. The filter loses a small part of the spectral signal, and the way its responses reach the network costs far more.

Summary

  • The outcome. On the 70-frame test split, the raw-band detector reaches recall 0.76 at IoU 0.5 and puts 76 % of the objects fully inside an exported region of interest. The detector on all 344 responses reaches 0.56 and 55 %; the one on the gate's top-10 voltages reaches 0.52 and 46 %.
  • Information is mostly kept. Per pixel, the 344 responses keep a median 93 % of the raw bands' object-versus-surround separability (80 to 99 % across 12 validation frames). What they lose is narrow spectral detail, such as the 790–800 nm red-edge feature that separates the object best in half of the frames. Each response is about 112 nm wide, against 3 nm bands.
  • The input format costs the most. Neighbouring voltages are 0.9998 cosine-similar, and 11 directions hold 99.9 % of the response energy. Fed those 344 near-copies, the detector took 37 epochs to reach val AP50 0.3, against 11 for the raw bands, and plateaued lower. The same responses re-expressed as 11 whitened principal directions keep 95 % of the separability, and a run on that input, still in progress, reached AP50 0.3 by epoch 5.
  • The learned top-10 is a weak subset. The gate's ten voltages form two bundles of near-identical curves and keep only 61 % of the separability. They also exclude 1.73–1.75 V, the single most discriminative response in 8 of 12 frames.

Where the camouflage signal lives

A camouflaged object is hard to see because its colour matches its surround. In the hyperspectral cube it usually still differs somewhere along the spectrum. Figure 1 shows, for one validation frame at a time, how visible the object becomes when each input is read out in the best possible single channel or linear combination. Figure 2 shows where along the spectrum, and along the filter's voltage axis, the difference sits.

What the EC filter does to a spectrum

Each of the 344 usable bias voltages gives the detector one number per pixel: the scene spectrum weighted by that voltage's responsivity curve. Figure 3 shows all 344 curves. They are broad (median 112 nm at half maximum, up to 311 nm), they change slowly with voltage, and they flip sign near 0.27 V. Figures 4 to 6 measure what that does to the information.

How it played out in training

All runs use YOLO26s at native 1680×1240 resolution with the same recipe: 100 epochs, batch 2 with 4-step accumulation, AdamW at 1e-4 with a cosine schedule. They differ only in the input. Session A feeds all 344 responses through a trainable weight vector (the gate). Session B keeps the gate's top-10 voltages and starts from session A's epoch-89 weights. The raw control feeds the 133 bands directly. The PCA-11 run feeds 11 whitened principal directions of the responses; it was started later and is still training.

What to change

  1. Feed a well-conditioned version of the responses, not 344 near-copies. The whitened 11-direction input keeps 95 % of the separability and learns at the raw model's pace. If the PCA-11 run ends near the raw model's plateau, adopt that input for Stage 2; the remaining gap is then the filter's bandwidth.
  2. Select voltages by information, not by the gate. For hardware that can read out only a few voltages, pick them greedily to maximise object–surround separability, or to span the principal directions. That would include 1.74 V and spread the choice across the voltage range, instead of two bundles of near-duplicates.
  3. Look past 800 nm. The best raw band is the last band of the window in 4 of 12 frames, so the red-edge signal likely continues into the near-infrared. The EC filter has no responsivity there, so this is a sensor-design question.
  4. Keep the raw control as the ceiling. The deployed sensor delivers responses, not bands, so the raw model is an upper bound on what any read-out of this scene data can reach with this detector and training set.

Methods and definitions

Data
HyperCOD cubes restricted to 400–800 nm (133 bands, 3.0 nm spacing), native 1680×1240 frames. 251 training, 28 validation and 70 test frames. Each frame is divided by its windowed 99th-percentile intensity.
EC responses
Responsivity from EC_filterV3.mat, 344 usable voltages from −1.00 to 2.50 V (0.25–0.31 V dead zone excluded), interpolated to the cube bands and L1-normalised per voltage. Response = responsivity-weighted sum of the bands, standardised with training-set band statistics.
Object and surround
Object pixels are the ground-truth mask eroded by 2 px. The surround is every pixel 3–60 px outside the mask, the background a camouflaged object has to blend into.
Fisher ratio
(meanobject − meansurround)² ÷ (varianceobject + variancesurround) for one channel.
Separability
Mahalanobis distance between the object and surround means under their pooled covariance, with a ridge of 10⁻³ of the mean eigenvalue. The best linear mix in Figure 1 is the matching discriminant direction.
Effective directions
Singular values of the 133×344 responsivity matrix (Figure 4, orange) and eigenvalues of the training-set band covariance (blue); k directions "hold" a fraction when the first k carry it.
Detector
YOLO26s pretrained on COCO, first convolution rebuilt for the input's channel count, NMS-free one-to-one head. Session A adds the gate N·softmax(θ) with learning rate 2e-2 and an entropy penalty.
Metrics
Recall and AP50 at IoU 0.5 over candidates with confidence ≥ 0.001. Exported boxes are those with confidence ≥ 0.02, at most 5 per frame; an object counts as inside an exported ROI when ≥ 99 % of its mask falls inside one box grown 1.5× (minimum 256 px).
Frames analysed
Figures 1, 2, 5 and 6: the first 12 validation frames. Figure 10: 12 test frames spaced evenly by object size.
